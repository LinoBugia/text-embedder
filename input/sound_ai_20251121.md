# Sound and AI: Speech, Music, Effects, and Multimodal Models

## 1. Background and overview

Sound is one of the most important modalities for real‑world AI systems. Modern models can:

- **Recognize speech** (automatic speech recognition, ASR).
- **Synthesize speech** from text (text‑to‑speech, TTS).
- **Understand non‑speech audio**, such as music, environmental sounds, and acoustic events.
- **Transform and remix audio**, including voice conversion, vocal separation, and sound‑effect generation.
- **Interact with users in real time**, via streaming ASR and low‑latency TTS.
- **Treat audio as tokens** that multimodal LLMs can read and generate, just like text.

At a high level, typical building blocks are:

- **Front‑end signal processing**: waveform → frames → spectrogram or learned features.
- **Encoders / decoders**: neural networks that map features to linguistic, musical, or acoustic representations.
- **Codecs and tokenizers**: neural audio codecs turn audio into discrete tokens for language models.
- **Transformers and sequence models**: model both audio sequences and mixed audio–text (and sometimes image) sequences.
- **Eval metrics**: word error rate (WER), character error rate (CER), BLEU/COMET for speech translation, MOS or objective metrics for TTS, and task‑specific metrics for music and sound (e.g. SDR for source separation, F1 for event detection).

Today’s ecosystem includes strong open‑source models (Whisper, Wav2Vec2, FastConformer, Zipformer, Paraformer, EnCodec‑based codecs, AudioLM‑style tokenizers, MusicGen‑family models, etc.), plus production APIs (Azure Speech, cloud ASR/TTS services) and local/edge solutions (sherpa‑onnx, Vosk, on‑device TTS, embedded classifiers).

This knowledge base summarizes how sound and AI connect across several themes:

1. ASR (Whisper and non‑Whisper) and speech‑centric audio.
2. Streaming ASR for real‑time use.
3. TTS, voice cloning, and practical deployment issues.
4. Audio tokens and multimodal LLMs.
5. Music, sound effects, and voice conversion.
6. Curated Hugging Face resources, models, and Spaces for sound.

It also highlights library changes (e.g. Hugging Face Datasets audio behavior) and “full‑pipeline” projects such as automated podcasts.

## 2. From official docs, courses, and papers

### 2.1 Speech recognition with Transformers and Whisper

Hugging Face’s Transformers library provides fully‑featured ASR pipelines. The `automatic-speech-recognition` pipeline accepts an audio file or NumPy array and returns text, with options for timestamps and chunking, and supports models like Whisper, Wav2Vec2, and FastConformer.

Key Whisper points from official docs and model cards:

- Whisper is a sequence‑to‑sequence Transformer trained on hundreds of thousands to millions of hours of speech for **ASR and speech translation**.
- The **`task`** generate argument controls whether the model performs transcription (`"transcribe"`) or translation (`"translate"`).
- The **`language`** argument specifies the language for multilingual models; newer decoding paths rely on `task` and `language` instead of legacy `forced_decoder_ids`.
- Model cards (for example, `openai/whisper-large-v3` or `openai/whisper-tiny`) document supported languages, dataset mix, and evaluation metrics such as WER and CER on standard benchmarks.

The Hugging Face Audio Course provides an accessible introduction to ASR and evaluation:

- **Word Error Rate (WER)** is the de‑facto metric for ASR, measuring substitutions, insertions, and deletions at the word level.
- **Character Error Rate (CER)** is often used for languages with logographic scripts or polysynthetic morphology, or when WER becomes less informative.
- The course walks through building and fine‑tuning ASR systems, then computing WER/CER reproducibly.

Official Whisper fine‑tuning guides show end‑to‑end training loops for multilingual ASR, including data preprocessing, feature extraction, training, and evaluation with the `Trainer` API.

### 2.2 Audio datasets and Hugging Face Datasets 3.6+

Hugging Face’s `datasets` library went through important changes between the 3.6.x and 4.x series:

- **3.6.x** was the last line where **Python dataset builder scripts** on the Hub were fully supported. It is often used as a “bridge” release to convert script‑based datasets into Parquet/Arrow.
- **4.x** fully removes dataset scripts from the loading path. Datasets that still relied on scripts (e.g. older ASR tutorials using `superb.py` or `librispeech_asr.py`) must be converted to Parquet/Arrow. Users otherwise see `RuntimeError: Dataset scripts are no longer supported, but found <script>.py`.
- **Media handling changed**: audio and video decoding is now delegated to TorchCodec + FFmpeg. Audio columns are decoded on access via `Audio` features, which requires installing the appropriate extras and having FFmpeg available.
- Users can opt out of automatic decoding with `Audio(decode=False)` or `dataset = dataset.cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))`, treating audio columns as file paths or raw bytes and decoding manually in dataloaders or collators.

Together, these changes push audio workloads toward **pure data + metadata** (Parquet/Arrow plus `dataset_infos.json`) and away from arbitrary Python execution in dataset scripts. This affects ASR projects that relied on old script‑based datasets and any audio pipeline that assumes SoundFile‑based decoding instead of TorchCodec.

### 2.3 Neural audio codecs and audio tokens

Several official explainers and papers describe **neural audio codecs** as “tokenizers for audio”:

- Kyutai’s explainer “Neural audio codecs: how to get audio into LLMs” frames the design as **sandwiching a language model between an encoder and decoder**: audio → codec encoder → discrete tokens → LLM → tokens → codec decoder → audio.
- Meta’s **EnCodec** is a state‑of‑-the‑art neural audio codec. Official docs and the “High Fidelity Neural Audio Compression” paper describe it as an autoencoder with a **residual vector quantization (RVQ) bottleneck** that produces **several parallel streams of audio tokens with a fixed vocabulary**.
- The AudioCraft documentation emphasizes that these tokens can be modeled with an **autoregressive language model**, exactly as if they were text tokens.

Core design patterns:

- Waveform is split into short frames, turned into spectrograms or learned features.
- An encoder compresses this sequence into lower‑rate latent vectors.
- A vector quantizer (VQ/RVQ/PQ) maps each latent to a codeword index in one or more codebooks.
- The sequence of indices is the **audio token sequence** fed to a Transformer or any sequence model.

### 2.4 AudioLM, semantic vs acoustic tokens, and surveys

The **AudioLM** paper and associated blog lay out a two‑tokenizer architecture:

- A **semantic tokenizer** (e.g. w2v‑BERT features discretized via k‑means) produces tokens capturing linguistic content.
- A **neural codec tokenizer** (e.g. SoundStream) produces tokens capturing acoustic detail such as timbre and prosody.

A stacked language model then predicts both token streams, enabling coherent long‑form audio generation.

The recent survey “Discrete Audio Tokens: More Than a Survey!” and associated benchmarks (e.g. DASB) systematize the space:

- They distinguish **codec tokens** (audio‑fidelity‑oriented, low bitrate) from **semantic tokens** (task‑oriented, often from SSL encoders).
- They evaluate trade‑offs between bitrate, intelligibility, and task performance for ASR, TTS, and other audio tasks.
- The survey positions audio tokenizers as first‑class citizens in multimodal LLMs, where text, images, and audio are all represented as discrete tokens.

### 2.5 Sound understanding beyond speech

Beyond classic ASR, several recent models position themselves as **audio foundation models** that jointly handle speech and non‑speech sounds:

- **SenseVoiceSmall** is a voice understanding model that performs **multilingual speech recognition, language identification, speech emotion recognition, and audio event detection** for Mandarin, Cantonese, English, Japanese, and Korean, with very low latency. It is provided as PyTorch and ONNX variants and can run on GPUs or specialized accelerators.
- SenseVoice‑style models treat voice as a rich signal that embeds **linguistic content, emotion, and acoustic events** in a single encoder, making them suitable for tasks such as call‑center analytics, meeting understanding, and multimodal assistants that react to both what is said and how it is said.
- Audio event detection capabilities blur the line between **speech** and **environmental sound recognition**, enabling models to recognize events like alarms, footsteps, or music segments alongside words and sentences.

These models show how “sound and AI” increasingly covers **speech, paralinguistic cues, and generic sound events** within a unified architecture.

## 4. From community, forums, and real‑world troubleshooting

### 4.1 Whisper fine‑tuning: loss vs WER and multilingual drift

Community discussions and practical notes highlight a common pattern when fine‑tuning Whisper on multilingual or domain‑specific data:

- **Cross‑entropy loss can keep decreasing while WER increases** on dev sets.
- Causes include **exposure bias** (teacher forcing vs free decoding), **decoding misconfiguration**, **normalization mismatch**, and **multilingual overfitting**.

Practically important points for Whisper:

- Use `generate(task="transcribe", language=<lang>, forced_decoder_ids=None)` instead of relying on config‑embedded `forced_decoder_ids`.
- Configure guardrails such as `compression_ratio_threshold`, `logprob_threshold`, and `no_speech_threshold`, and obey the requirement that temperature becomes a **list** when those thresholds are set.
- Use the **English** text normalizer only for English. For multilingual data, use the **Basic** normalizer or a locale‑aware normalizer; normalization can swing WER significantly.
- Monitor **per‑language WER** and consider **per‑language LoRA adapters** or experts to reduce catastrophic forgetting in multilingual setups.
- Early‑stop on **dev WER**, not just loss, and keep the best‑WER checkpoint.

These patterns come up repeatedly in ASR fine‑tuning reports and are critical for stable training.

### 4.3 Text‑to‑speech: Edge TTS, Azure Speech, and local TTS

Practical TTS deployments often start with **Microsoft Edge “Read Aloud” voices** via unofficial tools like `edge-tts`, but community reports show that:

- Microsoft introduced **short‑lived anti‑abuse tokens** and stricter filtering on datacenter IP ranges.
- From cloud environments (Colab, Hugging Face Spaces), connections may appear to succeed but **return no audio frames**, or end with WebSocket closes and HTTP 403 errors.
- Home PCs often still work because browsers fetch tokens directly and residential IPs are less likely to be filtered.

The recommended pivot is:

1. **Use official Azure Speech TTS** in cloud environments:
   - Same voice names (e.g. `en-US-AndrewMultilingualNeural`).
   - Stable, supported SDKs (`azure-cognitiveservices-speech`).
   - Straightforward integration into web apps and Spaces, with MP3 output for streaming or download.

2. **If Edge voices are required without Azure billing**, run a **personal relay** on a residential IP:
   - Projects like `openai-edge-tts` wrap Edge TTS in an OpenAI‑style API.
   - A Docker container on a home machine exposes a proxy endpoint that cloud apps call.

3. **Use fully local TTS** where appropriate:
   - Servers such as Kokoro TTS FastAPI or other neural TTS models can run on CPU or GPU.
   - These can be deployed in Spaces or self‑hosted environments with no external dependency.

Common advice:

- Stop repeatedly switching voices or shortening text when audio fails; the root cause is usually token/IP gating.
- Upgrade `edge-tts` when using it locally; the library has shipped multiple fixes in recent years.
- Log HTTP status codes and WebSocket frames to confirm whether you are hitting token or IP limits.

### 4.4 Audio tokens and multimodal LLMs in practice

Community posts, blogs, and re‑implementations (e.g. AudioLM in PyTorch, Kyutai’s nanoGPTaudio code) reinforce a standard pattern for audio tokens in multimodal LLMs:

- Waveform → **features** (spectrograms or encoder features).
- Features → **neural codec** (e.g. EnCodec, SoundStream) → discrete **codec tokens**.
- Optionally, waveform → **self‑supervised encoder** (e.g. w2v‑BERT) → discretization → **semantic tokens**.
- Tokens → **LLM** (either audio‑only or mixed with text tokens).
- LLM output tokens → codec decoder → waveform.

Projects built on EnCodec and related codecs (e.g. AudioCraft, MusicGen) demonstrate high‑quality reconstruction from token sequences and show how small Transformers can model token streams at moderate bitrates while staying faster than real time.

## 5. Implementation patterns and tips

### 5.1 Building robust ASR pipelines

For modern ASR pipelines with Transformers and Whisper‑like models:

- Use the **Transformers pipeline** for quick prototypes, then drop to the model’s `generate` interface for full control.
- Always set **`task` and `language`** explicitly for multilingual Whisper models.
- Disable legacy `forced_decoder_ids` and rely on `task`/`language` to avoid surprises during decoding.
- Add **guardrails**: `compression_ratio_threshold`, `logprob_threshold`, `no_speech_threshold`, and temperature lists to limit degenerate loops.
- Use the Hugging Face **`evaluate`** library’s WER/CER metrics for reproducible evaluation, and log **per‑language WER** in multilingual setups.
- Keep audio preprocessing consistent: sample rate (often 16 kHz), mono vs stereo, normalization, and segmentation strategy materially affect metrics.

For fine‑tuning:

- Start with **conservative learning rates** and freeze the encoder initially for Whisper‑style models.
- Add gradient clipping and warmup steps.
- Use **early stopping on dev WER**, with the best‑WER checkpoint as your main artifact.
- Consider **parameter‑efficient methods** (LoRA) for multilingual or domain‑specific specialization to reduce catastrophic forgetting.

### 5.2 Designing streaming ASR systems

Implementation tips for streaming ASR:

- Decide on **latency vs accuracy** trade‑offs: chunk size, right context, and look‑ahead.
- For RNNT‑based streaming (Emformer, FastConformer, Zipformer):
  - Follow reference configs for segment length and memory management.
  - Use provided streaming APIs and examples rather than writing custom loops from scratch.
- For blockwise Transformers (ESPnet, WeNet):
  - Tune block size and synchronous beam search parameters for your application.
- When integrating into apps:
  - Use WebSocket servers (sherpa‑onnx) or gRPC endpoints.
  - Implement partial result emission with stabilizing heuristics (e.g. only commit words older than a few frames).

### 5.3 Practical TTS deployment

For TTS in cloud or hybrid environments:

- Prefer **official, supported APIs** (Azure Speech, cloud vendor TTS) when reliability and legal compliance matter.
- Use **local TTS servers** (Kokoro, VITS‑based systems, Bark‑like models) when privacy and offline capability are critical.
- When deploying to Hugging Face Spaces or similar platforms:
  - Avoid directly calling browser‑only providers from the backend.
  - Store secrets (API keys) via environment variables or platform secrets.
  - Stream output audio where appropriate to minimize latency.

### 5.4 Working with audio in Hugging Face Datasets 4.x

To avoid surprises when using `datasets` for audio:

- Install the appropriate extras: `pip install "datasets[audio]"` to pull in TorchCodec and related dependencies, and ensure FFmpeg is available.
- Use `Audio(decode=False)` for inspection and when you want explicit control over decoding, especially in Colab/Spaces where TorchCodec may not be configured.
- For large ASR datasets:
  - Prefer **streaming mode** (`streaming=True`) for initial inspection and processing.
  - Convert to Parquet once, then use map‑style datasets for training.
- When migrating old script‑based datasets:
  - Use `datasets==3.6.x` in a dedicated environment to run conversion CLI tools.
  - Push converted Parquet/Arrow data and `dataset_infos.json` to the Hub and use `datasets>=4.x` thereafter.

### 5.5 Audio tokens and multimodal LLMs

When building or using models that treat audio as tokens:

- Choose an appropriate **neural codec** (e.g. EnCodec) with a bitrate and token rate that match your application’s constraints.
- Decide whether you need **semantic tokens**, codec tokens, or both:
  - Codec tokens for high‑fidelity TTS or audio continuation.
  - Semantic tokens for ASR‑like understanding or cross‑modal tasks.
- Pay attention to **token packing**:
  - Add modality tags and positional encodings.
  - Use separate embedding matrices per modality or a shared one with learned projections.
- Monitor both **objective metrics** (e.g. WER, MOS‑related predictors, SDR for separation) and **user experience** in interactive applications.

### 5.6 Music, sound effects, and voice conversion

For non‑speech sound applications:

- **Music generation**: Models such as MusicGen and Stable Audio use text and (optionally) melody or style prompts to generate music clips. Interactive Spaces make it easy to experiment with prompt engineering and style control.
- **Text‑to‑MIDI and symbolic music**: Datasets like MidiCaps pair MIDI files with natural‑language captions, enabling models that generate symbolic music from text or annotate music for search and recommendation.
- **Source separation and remixing**: Audio separation models (MDX/UVR, Demucs‑style networks) split tracks into vocals, drums, bass, and other stems. Spaces like “Audio Separator” provide Gradio frontends for quick experimentation.
- **Voice conversion and song covers**: RVC and SoVITS pipelines convert a reference voice into another timbre while preserving content and timing, powering song‑cover generators and VTuber pipelines.
- **Singing voice and stylistic TTS**: Fish‑Speech and similar models support multilingual, style‑controlled speech and singing, often with strong support for Japanese and English content.

Many of these systems can be combined: for example, generate a backing track with MusicGen, synthesize a vocal line with a TTS or singing model, and then post‑process the mix with separation or mastering tools.

## 6. Limitations, caveats, and open questions

Despite rapid progress, audio‑centric AI faces important limitations:

- **Data quality and bias**: ASR, TTS, and music models depend heavily on training data. Accents, dialects, under‑represented languages, and niche musical styles often perform significantly worse.
- **Robustness**: Background noise, reverberation, and overlapping speakers or instruments degrade performance. Specialized training, front‑end enhancement, or robust architectures may be required.
- **Latency and compute**: True streaming systems must optimize for low latency and stable partial outputs. Neural codecs and LLMs add computational overhead that may be challenging for edge devices.
- **Evaluation complexity**: WER/CER do not capture everything (e.g. semantic correctness, prosody, groove, or naturalness). Human evaluation (MOS) remains important for TTS; task‑specific metrics are needed for dialog, music, and sound‑effect systems.
- **Security and privacy**: Audio often contains sensitive information (voice biometrics, background conversations). Cloud‑hosted ASR/TTS raises privacy concerns; even local models must be secured.
- **Licensing and legal issues**: Some audio datasets and models have restrictive licenses or usage conditions (e.g. non‑commercial). Always verify dataset/model licenses before using them in commercial products.
- **Standardization of audio tokens**: There is still no universally adopted standard for audio tokens across toolkits and models. Interoperability between different codecs, language models, and music models remains an active research topic.

Open questions include:

- How to design **universal audio tokenizers** that are efficient, high‑quality, and broadly applicable across speech, music, and environmental sound.
- How to best combine **semantic and acoustic tokens** in multimodal LLMs.
- How to evaluate audio‑capable LLMs beyond simple ASR/TTS metrics (e.g. understanding of sound events, music theory, emotion, and interaction quality).
- How to build **resource‑efficient** yet high‑quality audio models for on‑device and edge deployment.
- How to manage **ethical and legal questions** around voice cloning, song covers, and deepfake audio.
